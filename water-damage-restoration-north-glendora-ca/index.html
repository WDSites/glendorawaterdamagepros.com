Water Damage Restoration in North Glendora, CA

Foothill homes below the San Gabriel Mountains face roof leaks and plumbing failures, and after wildfires, mud and runoff. Call (626) 879-0276 and describe what you see.

North Glendora is the foothill edge of Glendora, where streets climb toward the San Gabriel Mountains above Foothill Boulevard. City plans and local news reports use street names such as Sierra Madre Avenue, Glendora Mountain Road and Big Dalton Canyon Road for this part of town, and no official boundary for the neighborhood is published.

Most water damage here starts the way it does anywhere, with a failed supply line, a leaking water heater or a roof that let rain through. The foothill setting adds a second concern after wildfires, which is mud and runoff. This page keeps the two apart so you know which one you are dealing with.

What Is Documented About Water and the Foothills

The Colby Fire burned 1,992 acres above Glendora in January 2014, according to the City's draft Safety Element update. A USGS news article from February 2014 said the fire had increased debris flow hazard for the next three to five years, and that the city, Los Angeles County Public Works, the Forest Service and the National Weather Service met to plan for it.

Storms in 2014 then sent mud down some foothill streets. Local TV reports described mud running from Yucca Ridge toward Hicrest Road, mud reaching a property on Ridgeview Drive, and about four feet of mud against the back of a home in the 1100 block of Easley Canyon Road, some of it seeping inside. Los Angeles County crews cleared Glendora's foothill debris basins that year, with plans to take out as much as 45,000 cubic yards.

Those reports describe particular streets in a particular year. They do not show that every North Glendora street was affected, and we found no published record of debris flows reaching homes here in later years. The USGS window was three to five years from 2014, and we have not verified hillside conditions today.

About flood zones

The City's draft 2024 Safety Element says the built areas of Glendora are in FEMA Flood Zone X, which FEMA describes as an area of moderate or minimal hazard from the principal source of flood. That is not a promise that water cannot enter a home. The same document describes three concrete-lined flood channels crossing the city: Little Dalton Wash, Big Dalton Wash and San Dimas Wash. Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status not verified.

Everyday Causes Matter More Than the Mountain

For most calls the source is inside the house. A supply line lets go under a sink, a water heater seeps into a closet floor or rain finds a gap at a roof valley and shows up on a ceiling a few feet away. Foothill homes get the same plumbing failures as the rest of Glendora.

The city's specific plan for the former Monrovia Nursery site, in west-central Glendora between Sierra Madre Avenue and the railroad, describes the surrounding foothill neighborhood as late 20th century homes on generously shaded sites. It calls for 124 new detached homes on lots of at least 20,000 square feet, served by public water and sewer. Larger floor plans do not make leaks more likely, but one leak can reach more rooms.

Safety Steps When Water or Mud Reaches the House

Heed evacuation orders first. In 2014 the city and police issued mandatory evacuation orders for foothill areas and later lifted them. If an order covers your street, leave. The city's draft Safety Element lists evacuation routes that include Sierra Madre Avenue, Foothill Boulevard, Route 66, Glendora Avenue, Valley Center Avenue and Grand Avenue.

Do not wade into mud or moving water at the door. It can hide debris and may be contaminated, and cleanup treats it as the most contaminated category of water. Stay out of washes and flood channels. If water or mud is near outlets, appliances or the panel, do not touch the electrical system. Call 911 for immediate danger or the utility about a power or gas problem.

Close the main water valve if you can reach it dry, and photograph the damage from a safe spot. Household fans and shop vacuums are the wrong tools for mud or sewage.

  • Move vehicles, trash bins and other obstructions off the street if officials ask
  • Keep children and pets away from standing water and mud
  • Wait for the all clear before entering a muddy or flooded room
  • Do not start cleanup on your own while mud or water is still moving

Mud and Insurance

California's insurance department says flood damage sits outside a standard homeowners policy, and that flood and mudflow are commonly left out of commercial policies too. It describes mudflow as mud moving across ground that is usually dry, and says landslides and soil sliding down a slope are classed differently. Flood cover usually has to be bought on its own.

A new federal flood policy usually takes effect 30 days after purchase, so it cannot be bought on the day of a storm. Whether your situation is covered depends on your policy, so ask your insurer. Photos and moisture readings can document the damage, but they do not decide coverage.

How Cleanup Works

Water extraction comes first for mud and water, using pumps and extraction units, and affected porous materials such as carpet, pad and wet drywall are usually removed. Surfaces that stay are cleaned and dried with air movers and dehumidifiers while moisture meters show when the structure has reached dry standards. A company should be able to tell you whether its crews follow ANSI/IICRC S500, the published reference for this work.

Clean-water leaks are simpler. Water is extracted, wall and floor moisture is mapped with meters and a thermal camera, and structural drying equipment runs until readings match unaffected materials. The storm water damage and flood damage restoration pages cover the larger cases.

Before the Next Storm Season

None of this prevents damage, but it shortens the response. Know where your main water valve is and that it turns. Keep gutters and downspouts clear and aimed away from the house. Photograph each room and your belongings for your own records, and review your insurance for flood and mudflow exclusions.

If your street has had mud before, ask the city about storm alerts and evacuation routes for your address.
