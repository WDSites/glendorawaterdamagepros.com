Commercial water damage restoration is the work of stopping, removing and drying water in a building used for business, with the documentation and scheduling a working space needs. Dispatch answers 24/7, so call while you shut off the supply and move people away from wet floors.

This page covers what we do on a commercial loss: water extraction, structural drying, removal of wet materials, containment and a written scope. It also says plainly where other trades and your own decisions come in.

What Makes a Commercial Loss Different

A commercial space usually has larger floor areas, shared walls, longer hours of use and more people who need access. Water travels the same way it does in a house, but the cost of every wet day is higher because the space cannot sit idle.

The causes are familiar: a supply line failure, a roof leak, a clogged drain, an HVAC condensate line or a backed-up restroom. We cannot give local figures on which is most common in Glendora, because no verified local source tracks it.

When you call, give the street, the nearest cross street and the entrance crews should use. Glendora's main routes, Route 66 and Foothill Boulevard, are easy to name, but a suite number or side door tells crews exactly where to go.

Safety Steps Before Anyone Calls Us

Move people out of wet areas and keep them away from outlets, panels and equipment sitting in water. Shut off power to the affected area only when you can reach the panel on dry footing. Sparking, a burning smell or gas means 911 or the utility gets the first call, ahead of ours.

A leaking fire sprinkler line is a fire protection matter and needs that system's own contractor to isolate it. Sewage means everyone stays out of the area until it is contained. Call 911 first only if there is an immediate hazard.

How a Commercial Job Is Run

Before hiring, find out if a contractor's crew follows ANSI/IICRC S500, which is a published trade standard, and where its job notes are kept.

  1. Walk the space and write the scope

    We follow the water using moisture meters and a thermal camera, then write down the rooms, materials and work involved. Before work begins, you hear what the job involves and what it costs.

  2. Photograph and log

    Dated photos and readings are taken before anything is moved, so the record shows the loss as found.

  3. Protect and move contents

    Furniture, stock and equipment are lifted, wrapped or moved so wet floors can be reached. Anything that cannot be saved is listed before it is discarded.

  4. Extract and remove wet materials

    Extraction units pull standing water, and board, insulation, pad and tiles that cannot dry are cut out.

  5. Contain and filter where needed

    Containment barriers separate the work zone from the rest of the building. Negative air machines with HEPA filtration are added when demolition is dusty or contamination is involved.

  6. Dry and monitor

    Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers run while we take readings at marked points until the materials reach their dry reference.

Questions That Decide the Plan

Four answers shape most commercial scopes, and you can give them on the first call.

Can part of the space stay open?

Sometimes a dry zone can stay in use behind containment barriers. Heavy extraction and demolition may need the wet area closed.

Is the water clean, gray or black?

Clean supply water is frequently dried where it sits, while sewage means removal of porous materials and cleaning.

Which materials are wet?

Concrete slab, board, ceiling tile and carpet tile each dry or fail differently.

Who decides on the repair?

The building owner, whoever holds the lease and the insurer may each have a say. Settle who authorizes work before demolition starts.

Records That Support a Business Claim

Photograph the source, the water line on walls and every affected room before cleanup. Keep a list of damaged contents, stock and equipment with dates, and hold on to receipts for emergency work.

We supply a written scope and the daily moisture log from the job. Those records describe what happened and what was done. They do not decide whether a policy pays, and approval is between you and your insurer.

Drying While People Still Use the Building

Drying equipment is loud, cords cross walkways, and warm air from dehumidifiers changes the feel of a room. Containment barriers and a planned equipment layout keep most of that inside the work zone.

Some losses cannot stay in use. If water is widespread or contaminated, the honest plan is to close that area until readings are dry. The scope you get before work starts says which parts stay open.

What changes the scope and price

A commercial scope grows with floor area, wet days and how much of the building must stay accessible.

Water category

The water's source, clean supply line or gray or black contamination, decides which materials stay and which go.

Affected area

Square footage, shared walls and the number of rooms set how much equipment is needed.

Materials involved

Slab, ceiling tile, board and floor coverings each need different handling.

Drying days and equipment

Daily equipment use and monitoring accumulate until readings reach target.

Rebuild scope

Finishes, ceilings and flooring that must be replaced are separate from mitigation.

Business property coverage can apply when a pipe bursts or equipment fails abruptly. Slow leaks and deferred maintenance are the kind of loss insurers often decline, and flooding or mudflow normally calls for its own flood policy.

Mistakes to Avoid After a Business Water Loss

A few quick decisions cause most of the trouble.

  • Throwing out contents before they are listed and photographed.
  • Re-occupying a room because the carpet looks dry.
  • Treating restoration as a fix for the leak itself. The pipe, roof or equipment needs the right licensed trade.
  • Waiting to see if it dries, since porous materials wet beyond 48 hours often need replacing.
